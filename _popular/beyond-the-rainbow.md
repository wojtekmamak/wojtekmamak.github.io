**Outline:**

This article explores the fundamental question of what it means to discover a "new" color, tracing the boundary between the physics of light, the chemistry of pigments, and the neurology of perception. Starting with Isaac Newton's macabre experiments and the chemical synthesis of *YInMn Blue*, I argue that for centuries, finding new colors merely meant finding new chemical ways to trigger the exact same perceptual experiences. 

To understand true perceptual novelty, the piece delves into the philosophy of color—specifically neurophenomenal structuralism—and the biological limits of our trichromatic vision. The narrative climaxes with a groundbreaking 2025 experiment at Berkeley, where scientists used precise lasers to bypass optical physics and directly stimulate individual retinal cones, allowing humans to see *olo*—a color that does not exist in nature, cannot be printed, and cannot be displayed on any screen. 

Ultimately, I combine these scientific breakthroughs with Ludwig Wittgenstein's philosophy of language to show that while technology can now hack our visual hardware to produce entirely unprecedented neurological events, we remain trapped by a vocabulary built exclusively for a natural world.
