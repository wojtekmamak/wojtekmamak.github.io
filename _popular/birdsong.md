***[Tygodnik Powszechny, no. 27/2026]***

**Outline:**

This article looks at how artificial intelligence is transforming the study of birdsong. For decades, researchers had to squint at spectrograms of songs recorded in soundproof lab chambers; wild soundscapes were simply too noisy. Language-model-style systems such as TweetyBERT, trained on vast song libraries much like ChatGPT is trained on text, can now discover structure in field recordings on their own — revealing, for instance, that birds seem to obey Zipf's law, using the shortest syllables for their most frequent signals.

Mapping a "grammar" is only half the job, though; the harder question is meaning. Here the black-capped chickadee is the star witness: the number of "D notes" in its alarm call tracks the danger posed by a predator, with a small, agile pygmy-owl triggering a long string and a large, slow great horned owl only one or two. Along the way, I discuss why lab favourites like the zebra finch can mislead, how pandemic lockdowns changed urban song, how song is learned in a critical window (and what Olga Fehér's isolated finches reveal about its genetic template), and why birds sing so much when no mate or rival is listening.

Finally, I turn to the brain: although birds and mammals parted ways some 320 million years ago and birds lack a layered neocortex, work by Erich Jarvis and others shows that the circuits for vocal learning in songbirds and humans share strikingly similar structure and gene expression — which makes birds an unexpectedly good mirror for the origins of human language.
